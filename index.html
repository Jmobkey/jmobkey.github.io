Check your project before you scale it.

The production readiness review is eighteen statements across the six areas where AI projects stall after a good pilot. The verdict follows your weakest link, not your average, and it tells you where to start.

It works for any industry, takes about five minutes, and runs in your browser.

2026 —Product
Phase I shipped

Smart Tools

A suite of AI and logic-driven utilities that let trade customers identify and source the right part without phoning a branch. Phase I shipped four, including a cross-referencer that reads a photo of an equipment data plate and returns compatible replacements.

The design problem throughout is that a near-match is worse than no answer, because someone will fit it. Substitution logic stays deterministic and rule-bound; the model handles extraction and disambiguation, never the compatibility call.

My part: architected and implemented the functional logic, calculation engines and AI integrations. Ran structured beta testing with staff and volunteer customers, then worked with the web team to port it into production.

2024 —Programme
Grassroots to mandate

Getting AI adopted from the bottom up

The first AI workflows at the company were unofficial. A colleague and I built them on our own time with no mandate and no budget, on the theory that the fastest route to a decision was a working thing rather than a deck.

That ran as an on-call sideline for the better part of a year before it earned a four-month pilot, during which I split each week between branch duties and building. The pilot became a full-time role, then a national remit, then this one.

Why it's here: most AI strategy work is executing a mandate somebody else secured. This is the record of manufacturing one from a branch counter.

Where the line goes

Walk a workflow step by step and decide which parts can be probabilistic and which must stay deterministic. Each step gets a call on who makes the final decision: a rule, a person or the model.

Automation architecture

Working standards for automation that gets depended on: idempotency, retry semantics, dead lettering, and alerting on silence rather than only on failure. Four patterns with the failure mode of each, and an honest account of when to move off a no-code platform, including the bad reasons people usually give.

A gas code evaluation set and a field-conditions reference architecture are in progress.

I learned this trade in mechanical rooms.

Six years as an HVAC-R service technician, a Red Seal gasfitter ticket out of NAIT, then a stretch behind the counter quoting jobs for contractors. I'm finishing a BSc in Computer Science alongside the work, conferring at the end of 2026.

What the field gives you is a low tolerance for answers that are nearly right. Knowing which parts of a system can be probabilistic and which have to stay deterministic is most of the design work, and knowing where that line sits comes from having been the person holding the wrong part.
